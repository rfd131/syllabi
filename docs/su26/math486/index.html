Welcome to MATH 486: Mathematical Theory of Games

The Summer 2026 semester of MATH 486 is an asynchronous online course. We hope you will find this to be an engaging and enjoyable online course.

Course Format: Asynchronous online, running May 18 – Aug 12, 2026. The course is structured as 10 weekly lessons. Each lesson includes a set of pre-recorded lecture videos, textbook readings, a written homework assignment, a reflection assignment, and a quiz. Written homeworks include exercises and problems from the current lesson. Quizzes typically review content from the previous lesson. There is one "take home" midterm reviewing material from the first half of the semester, one "take-home" final reviewing material from the second half of the semester, and an in-person proctored final exam at the end of the semester.

Course Overview

Game theory is a mathematical framework for studying strategic interaction. It is a wide-ranging subject with numerous interesting applications including auction and market design, evolutionary biology, distributed systems and network design, optimization, computer science, environmental and resource management policy, and political science. We will focus on two main areas: non-cooperative game theory (Lessons 1–7) and evolutionary game theory (Lessons 8–10). In our study of non-cooperative game theory we will motivate concepts and ideas with basic applications, develop the abstract mathematical theory from these motivating examples, and then apply and extend the theory to a variety of examples. As we examine real applications we will discuss some of the limitations of the theory. In our coverage of evolutionary game theory we will be interested in applications to biology. This topic will require some use of modeling with differential equations. While some background in differential equations is helpful, it is not a prerequisite for the course; we will develop the material we need from differential equations as part of the course (the coverage is similar to the differential-equations material in Math 141B).

We'll rely on the required text, pre-recorded lectures, interactive components (including interactive games where extra credit may be on the line), a discussion forum (Piazza), and supplemental materials. Your active participation is what will make this asynchronous online course a successful and enjoyable experience.

A note on workload: Written homework accounts for a substantial part of the overall grade. Homework is designed to help you engage with and learn the course content, and there are opportunities for revision so you can revisit misunderstandings. Consistent participation, following up on revision opportunities, posting substantive questions in Piazza, and making use of office hours are the best ways to get the most out of this course.

Prerequisites

  • Math 220 (Matrices)
  • Math 140 (Calculus I)

Background skills

  • We will develop carefully reasoned mathematical arguments. If you have taken a proof-based course this will feel familiar, but proof-writing experience is not a prerequisite; we aim to develop these skills throughout the semester.
  • We will sometimes need to solve linear (and less frequently nonlinear) systems of equations. We assume methods for this are familiar or can be reviewed as needed.
  • We will frequently rely on differential calculus to find maxima or minima or to determine when a function is increasing or decreasing in a given variable.
  • To analyze dynamical systems that arise in evolutionary game theory, we will develop tools from linear algebra and differential equations: determinants, eigenvalues, and eigenvectors; linearization of nonlinear systems near equilibria; and phase plane analysis of two-dimensional nonlinear systems.

Course Format (weekly)

  • Pre-recorded Lectures: Recorded videos covering material and worked examples.
  • Written Homework: Each lesson includes a written homework assignment with a short set of exercises (practice with definitions and concepts) and a problem set (extending and applying the material). Submitted in Gradescope.
  • Quiz: Weekly quizzes posted in the Math 486 web app and submitted in Gradescope. Quizzes typically review content from the previous lesson.
  • Online Office Hours: Drop in via Zoom during scheduled office hours, or schedule an appointment.
  • Interactive Participation: Reflection assignments (weekly) and interactive games during some weeks of the semester. Some games involve being randomly paired with a peer; others involve interaction with the entire class. Participation contributes to your overall grade and gives you the opportunity to earn extra credit.
