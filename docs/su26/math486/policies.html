Academic Integrity

Academic dishonesty is not limited to cheating on an exam or assignment. The following is quoted directly from the PSU Faculty Senate Policies for Students regarding academic integrity:

Academic integrity is the pursuit of scholarly activity free from fraud and deception and is an educational objective of this institution. Academic dishonesty includes, but is not limited to, cheating, plagiarizing, fabricating of information or citations, facilitating acts of academic dishonesty by others, having unauthorized possession of examinations, submitting work of another person or work previously used without informing the instructor, or tampering with the academic work of other students.

All University and Eberly College of Science policies regarding academic integrity apply to this course. See the Eberly College of Science integrity policies for further details.

Use of Generative AI

Submitting work produced by generative technology such as ChatGPT or Copilot as a substitute for work that represents your own effort and understanding is a clear violation of academic integrity.

Appropriate use of generative AI

Generative AI can be appropriately used to help you review and understand concepts and definitions. Example prompts where ChatGPT can provide useful responses include: "What is the difference between perfect and imperfect information in an extensive form game?", "How is imperfect information represented in a game tree?", "What is a mixed strategy?", and "What are some of the most important learning objectives in an introductory game theory course?". Using AI to explore prompts of this kind is appropriate and can assist your learning.

Inappropriate use of generative AI

  • Most of the benefit from this course comes from your personal effort in solving problems. There is no substitute for that work.
  • Generative AI is particularly problematic where the user does not have the expertise to evaluate the AI's output. The work may look correct, but the AI does not know the meaning of what it produces (sometimes called the 'illusion of correctness').
  • Generative technologies produce solutions that may sound convincing or reasonable to a student, but frequently contain errors that are obvious to an expert and very different from the errors students typically make. Reliance on generative AI is easy for instructors to spot and difficult for students to understand.

Students facing allegations of academic misconduct may not drop or withdraw from the affected course unless they are cleared of wrongdoing (see G-9: Academic Integrity). Attempted drops will be prevented or reversed, and students will be expected to complete coursework and meet course deadlines.
